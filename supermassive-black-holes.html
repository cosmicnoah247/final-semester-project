Supermassive Black Hole Anatomy

Supermassive black holes are the largest type of black hole, containing millions to billions of times the mass of our Sun. They reside at the centers of most galaxies, including our own Milky Way, and play a crucial role in galactic evolution.

These behemoths form through the gradual accretion of gas, dust, and stars over billions of years, or through the merger of smaller black holes during galaxy collisions. Their immense gravity warps spacetime on a galactic scale.

Supermassive black holes can power quasars and active galactic nuclei, emitting intense radiation and relativistic jets that influence star formation and galaxy structure. They also act as gravitational anchors, stabilizing galactic disks.

While supermassive black holes grow over cosmic time, they may eventually evaporate through Hawking radiation, though this process takes far longer than the current age of the universe, potentially trillions of years.
